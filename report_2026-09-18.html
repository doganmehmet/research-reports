The Hormuz Shock and the Un-Inverting Curve: Navigating the Late-Cycle Trap in US Growth Equities

Over the past three months leading up to 18 September 2026, global markets have been violently repriced by a resurgence in inflationary pressures and geopolitical supply shocks, most notably the breakdown of the Memorandum of Understanding over the Strait of Hormuz lseg.com. While the MSCI World Index and AI-driven US equities initially showed resilience, the subsequent surge in Brent crude and distillate shortages have reignited inflation fears, prompting the Federal Reserve to hike rates by 25 basis points to 4.0% on 17 September 2026 reuters.com. This hawkish pivot triggered a nine-month high in global equity outflows, even as gold surged to $4,415.90 per ounce as of 31 August 2026 and the 10-year Treasury yield retreated to 4.88% as of 31 August 2026 ftinvest.us. The resulting macroeconomic backdrop—characterized by sticky inflation, slowing growth, and a newly un-inverted yield curve—bears a striking resemblance to the late-cycle stagflationary traps of the 1970s and the yield-curve un-inversion signals of 2000.

When we examine the current US macroeconomic setup, the parallels to the 1973 and 1978 stagflationary episodes are difficult to ignore. In the late 1970s, geopolitical conflicts in the Middle East severely constrained energy supplies, driving inflation back up just as economic growth stalled. Today, the Strait of Hormuz disruptions have created severe distillate shortages, pushing the US CPI back to 3.4% as of 31 August 2026, while GDP slumped to just 1.5% in the second quarter of 2026. The Fed’s decision to raise the target rate to 4.0% on 17 September 2026 mirrors the historical tightening cycles where central banks were forced to crush demand to fight supply-side inflation. For growth equities, which currently trade at a trailing P/E of 26.07 as of 31 August 2026, this environment is historically toxic; rising discount rates and margin compression devastate long-duration assets during oil shocks.

Another critical historical parallel lies in the Treasury market’s behavior and the broader economic fragility it exposes. After a prolonged period of inversion, the 10-year yield has crossed back above the 2-year yield, resulting in a positive 36 basis point spread as of 31 August 2026. In early 2001, following the dot-com peak, the yield curve similarly un-inverted as the economy broke under the weight of prior tightening. Currently, US unemployment has ticked up to 4.2% as of 31 August 2026, and household debt service payments sit at 11.16% of disposable income as of 31 March 2026, indicating that the consumer is increasingly strained. Growth stocks, heavily reliant on pristine economic expansion and cheap terminal capital, face severe multiple compression when the market realizes the yield curve un-inversion is a signal of economic breakage rather than a return to healthy expansion.

Despite these macroeconomic warning signs, the Nasdaq 100’s climb to 29,644.17 as of 31 August 2026 reflects a desperate reach for AI-driven earnings growth, echoing the narrow market breadth seen in late 1999. However, with the VIX sitting at a deceptively low 14.81 as of 31 August 2026, equity investors appear dangerously sanguine about the impending earnings revisions. The massive $31.44 billion outflow from US equity funds in the week ending 16 September 2026 suggests that institutional capital is beginning to recognize the lagging risks of the Fed’s tightening cycle reuters.com.

When we overlay the current S&P 500 P/E ratio of 26.07 as of 31 August 2026 against historical late-cycle peaks, the valuation buffer is remarkably thin given the resurgent inflation. In 1973, before the bear market truly deepened, multiples contracted sharply as the reality of a sluggish economy and high inflation set in. We are witnessing the early stages of this de-rating, as the market transitions from pricing in a soft landing to pricing in a stagflationary slowdown.

Based on these historical patterns, we must carefully weigh the specific threats and potential avenues for capital preservation and growth.

  • Risk: Stagflationary Margin Compression. If Brent crude remains elevated near $98.77 as of 31 August 2026 due to ongoing Middle East supply constraints, input costs will surge while consumer demand softens. Growth companies with high operating leverage and unproven pricing power will face severe earnings downgrades as their margins are squeezed from both sides.
  • Risk: Yield Curve Un-Inversion Recession Signal. The positive 10Y-2Y spread historically acts as a lagging indicator of recession onset. If unemployment continues its upward trajectory from 4.2%, the resulting drop in corporate earnings will disproportionately punish high-multiple tech and AI beneficiaries that require flawless execution to justify their valuations.
  • Opportunity: Energy and Commodity Beneficiaries within Tech. While traditional growth suffers, AI infrastructure requires massive physical resources. Companies bridging the gap between digital growth and physical commodities—such as power generation, grid infrastructure, and materials—offer a hedge against the very inflation that is hurting the broader tech sector.
  • Opportunity: Quality Growth with Pricing Power. As the market rotates away from speculative, long-duration AI plays, capital will likely flow toward mega-cap growth companies with fortress balance sheets and inelastic demand. These firms can pass through inflationary costs to consumers, protecting their margins in a 3.4% CPI environment.

Navigating this environment requires a disciplined approach to risk management, acknowledging that the macroeconomic backdrop is shifting from a disinflationary boom to an inflationary slowdown.

  • Suggested Portfolio Exposure: 25-50%
  • Justification: The confluence of a geopolitical oil shock, resurgent inflation, and a yield curve that has un-inverted amidst slowing GDP creates a highly hostile environment for broad growth equity exposure. While the AI narrative remains intact, the macroeconomic backdrop strongly resembles the late-cycle traps of 1973 and 2000, where multiple compression is inevitable. Maintaining a 25-50% allocation allows for participation in quality, cash-flowing tech leaders while preserving enough dry powder to capitalize on the inevitable valuation reset when the lagging effects of the Fed’s 4.0% rate hike fully materialize.
